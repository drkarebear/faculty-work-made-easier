About the project

Useful things for real faculty work.

Faculty Work Made Easier is an independent collection of practical resources designed to reduce the friction around common faculty tasks.

About Karen Crozer

Karen Crozer, Ph.D., is a California Community Colleges professor, department chair, and faculty-development practitioner who likes building useful things.

Her work spans open education, accessibility, instructional technology, AI, curriculum, and faculty support. Faculty Work Made Easier grew out of a recurring experience: faculty often spend too much time navigating processes, finding information, or recreating tools that could be simpler.

The resources here are independent faculty-created projects designed to solve small but real problems and give instructors more time for the work that matters most.

Karen is also the ASCCC OERI English Discipline Lead for California and Chair of English, Communication Studies, and Journalism at Los Angeles Mission College. Views and resources on this site are her own.

What guides the project

  • Useful before impressive. The goal is to make the next step clearer, not to make the tool more complicated.
  • Human judgment stays central. AI can assist with searching, sorting, and first-pass work, but people still make consequential decisions.
  • Accessibility is part of design. Accessibility is treated as a design requirement rather than an afterthought.
  • Independent does not mean infallible. Corrections, broken-link reports, and accessibility feedback are welcome.

Accessibility

I aim to make Faculty Work Made Easier usable by as many people as possible and to meet or exceed WCAG 2.2 Level AA where this project controls the experience. The site uses semantic headings and landmarks, keyboard-accessible navigation, visible focus indicators, high-contrast text and controls, responsive reflow, large interaction targets, reduced-motion support, and a dedicated dark-mode palette.

External tools and websites linked from this hub are maintained separately and may have their own accessibility features or barriers. Automated testing cannot certify accessibility by itself, so human review, keyboard testing, zoom/reflow checks, and assistive-technology testing remain part of the process.
